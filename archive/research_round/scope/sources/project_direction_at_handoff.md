# The larger scientific goal

The goal is to explain the geometry of balanced network activity from the distribution of local neuronal dynamics. Balance creates different operating points even when neurons share the same microscopic parameters within a cell class. A useful collective theory should retain those differences without requiring a separate dynamical calculation for every neuron.

The present construction uses conditional input processes. A neuron's input is Gaussian conditional on its fixed mean, while the centered input covariance concentrates within a population under the stated assumptions. Passing that input through a nonlinear rate function produces distributed means, variances, temporal kernels, and responses. The collective calculation depends on joint moments of these quantities. Replacing the distribution by an average neuron before taking those moments can change the predicted dimension substantially.

The broader aim is a map from a distribution of local trajectory laws to collective correlation geometry. E and I labels specify populations, and the quenched operating point specifies variation within each population. Additional labels should enter when a change in the connectivity ensemble or dynamics requires them. A mean and a standard deviation alone do not specify a Gaussian process; its temporal covariance kernel remains part of the description.

## What is established at the present scope

The conditional spectral moments enter a small population response resolvent. Integrating its covariance energy gives participation ratio and separate predictions for E-E, E-I, and I-I correlations over two lags. The scalar reduction also explains the direction of error from two premature averaging approximations. Near a simple smooth transition, the conditional theory reduces to a critical mode and explicit coefficients.

Population composition and degree variability have distinct roles. At fixed aggregate input matrices, E:I fractions reweight covariance geometry within the theory. Degree dispersion changes static input disorder and the operating-point distribution. The completed simulations support the large degree effect and the dominant 80:20 lag structure. The explicit separation specifies what needs to change when a control parameter changes.

The connection to prior work should remain clear. Clark and collaborators provide the two-site approach, heterogeneous spectral products, and the local-response viewpoint. Balanced EI mean-field work supplies the one-site foundation and earlier accounts of degree-dependent static disorder. The target here is their conditional combination for balance-generated heterogeneity, together with tests that distinguish algebraic reductions from assumptions about nonlinear networks. The references and reading notes record that relationship; no broad priority claim is made.

## The next useful scientific decision

The 60:40 failure is the most informative unresolved comparison. Holding aggregate coupling fixed preserves local input laws within the conditional calculation, but the measured static and dynamic variances depart from those laws. The dimension mismatch could reflect local-law error, pair-closure error, finite-size effects, recording effects, or several of these. Current evidence does not establish the cause.

A focused investigation should hold the control path and estimator fixed. Compare predicted and measured local means, variances, temporal kernels, and gain moments first. Then compare the pair calculation using predicted local statistics with a diagnostic using measured local statistics. The latter diagnoses where error enters and must remain separate from the parameter-only prediction. Longer recordings and a small independently replicated size series can test whether the discrepancy decreases. Specify the runs before inspecting agreement and retain every realization.

A satisfactory resolution would identify a failing assumption and either predict its correction or establish a supported domain of validity. Fitting a covariance amplitude after seeing the data would not provide that explanation. Agreement near 80:20 also cannot establish a biological optimum. The analytic fraction optimum depends on the specified control path and critical law.

## Extensions that preserve the central question

The next degree experiment should use the nonuniform variance profile, where E and I have different local laws. Its conditional predictions already exist, while the completed degree-network validation uses the uniform profile. This is a direct test of whether the proposed degree mechanism transfers across population structure.

The current degree parameter describes count fluctuations of order the square root of population size. Broad relative degree heterogeneity is a different limit. Incoming degree or strength may then need to remain as a conditional neuronal label, and input variances may cease to concentrate within a class. The natural extension is a distribution over conditional kernels and responses, derived from the connectivity ensemble.

Higher covariance moments are a later goal. Participation ratio uses only the first two trace moments. It does not determine the eigenvalue density or the alignment of E and I subspaces. A multiple-site derivation must retain repeated-neuron moments and control correlations among nonlinear residuals. The fixed-connectivity representation offers a candidate route, but its present errors prevent treating it as a validated spectral theory. A targeted third-moment or leading-eigenvalue test would give a clearer next result than an unsupported full-spectrum claim.

Transitions also require distinctions. A bulk chaotic instability, coherent E/I population instability, and divergent static susceptibility are different events. The current critical laws assume common leak times, an isolated smooth critical mode, nonzero curvature, and stable coherent feedback. Unequal timescales or nonsmooth transfer functions require new derivations and direct onset tests.

## A useful stopping criterion

A strong next version would contain one transparent conditional construction, a verified account of its limits, and a small set of discriminating simulations. The operating-point distribution should explain both successful and failed predictions. Once the fraction discrepancy and the nonuniform degree test are settled, the pair theory can stand as a coherent result even if the full covariance spectrum remains a separate project.
